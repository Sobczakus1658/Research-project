\section{Related Work}\label{sec:related}

\subsection{AI-assisted and agentic software development}

Research on large-language-model support for programming initially focused on interactive code-completion tools. Barke et al. identified two recurring modes of interaction with such tools: developers use them either to accelerate work whose solution they already understand or to explore possible solutions when the next step is uncertain \cite{barke2023grounded}. In a controlled user study, Vaithilingam et al. found that GitHub Copilot did not consistently improve task completion time or success, although participants valued it as a starting point; understanding, editing, and debugging generated snippets remained important obstacles \cite{vaithilingam2022expectation}. Evidence about productivity is therefore mixed and context dependent. Peng et al. reported a substantial speed-up on a bounded implementation task \cite{peng2023impact}, whereas Becker et al. found that early-2025 AI tools increased completion time by 19\% for experienced open-source developers working on tasks in mature projects they knew well \cite{becker2025impact}. Together, these studies show that the effects of AI assistance cannot be inferred from model capability alone: they also depend on the user, task, codebase, and verification effort.

Coding agents extend this interaction model from local suggestions to repository-level actions and complete pull requests. Watanabe et al. studied 567 Claude Code pull requests across 157 open-source projects and found that 83.8\% were eventually merged; among the merged contributions, 45.1\% still required human revisions \cite{watanabe2025agentic}. Li et al. subsequently introduced AIDev, a substantially larger dataset covering pull requests created through several coding agents and enabling population-level studies of agentic development on GitHub \cite{li2026aidev}. Studies based on AIDev have begun to examine not only generated changes but also the human workflows surrounding them. Gao et al. observed that AI-co-authored pull requests were frequently submitted by contributors without prior code ownership and were often merged with little explicit feedback \cite{gao2026autopilot}. Peralta et al. further showed that merge and rejection labels alone are insufficient indicators of agent performance: only 35.7\% of rejected Agentic-PRs in their manually examined sample represented clear agent failures, while workflow constraints and absent decision rationales accounted for the remaining cases \cite{peralta2026decisions}. These findings motivate an interaction-aware view in which acceptance and review discussion describe different aspects of an agent-associated contribution.

\subsection{Pull-request acceptance and code-review discussion}

The interpretation of pull-request outcomes has long been studied independently of AI. Gousios et al. showed that pull-request decisions and processing time are associated with a relatively small set of contribution-, project-, and contributor-level factors \cite{gousios2014pull}. Tsay et al. demonstrated that evaluators also use visible social information and prior relationships in addition to the technical characteristics of a contribution \cite{tsay2014influence}. More recent ecosystem-level evidence likewise indicates that experience acquired through prior contributions, issue participation, and collaboration can improve pull-request acceptance, including for newcomers to a particular project \cite{meijer2025ecosystem}. Consequently, the acceptance of an Agentic-PR cannot be treated as a direct measure of either the generated code's intrinsic quality or the developer's ability: it is an outcome produced jointly by the change, its author, the repository, and its maintainers.

Review comments require similar caution. Modern code review serves several purposes beyond defect detection, including knowledge transfer, awareness, discussion of alternatives, and enforcement of project conventions \cite{bacchelli2013expectations}. Developers' perceptions of review quality also depend on the usefulness and thoroughness of feedback, reviewer familiarity with the change, and characteristics of the submitted code \cite{kononenko2016quality}. A larger number of comments may therefore indicate requested corrections, but it may also reflect a complex task, an active review culture, clarification, or design discussion. Conversely, a pull request with no comments may have been straightforward, weakly scrutinized, or handled through interactions that are not visible in the recorded discussion. For this reason, the present study uses comment count as a measure of \emph{review-discussion volume}, not as a direct measure of corrections, review effort, or code quality.

\subsection{Developer experience in studies of agentic contributions}

Developer experience is a latent construct rather than a GitHub field. Repository-mining studies have represented it through signals such as account tenure, prior commits, accepted pull requests, project participation, technical skills, and collaboration history. Dey et al., for example, modeled developer expertise in an ecosystem-wide ``Skill Space'' rather than reducing it to activity in a single repository \cite{dey2021expertise}. This line of work supports multidimensional representations, but it also underlines an important validity limitation: observable platform activity is only a proxy for experience and may omit professional work performed outside public repositories.

The closest study to ours is that of Asdaque et al., who also analyzed AIDev through the lens of developer experience \cite{asdaque2026novice}. They examined 22,953 Agentic-PRs submitted by 1,719 developers and divided users into two groups according to lifetime commits normalized by account age. Their lower-experience group submitted larger changes, received 4.52 times as many review comments, had a 31\% lower acceptance rate, and waited 5.16 times longer for resolution. This provides direct evidence that developer characteristics are associated with outcomes of agent-supported contributions. At the same time, its binary, single-indicator operationalization leaves open whether the pattern persists under a broader representation of experience and in a larger developer population.

Our study complements, rather than duplicates, this work. We combine AIDev with multiple GitHub activity indicators and derive three relative profiles through unsupervised clustering. We also study agent-use intensity, which is not captured by comparing only the characteristics of submitted pull requests. Finally, we analyze acceptance and review discussion separately and avoid treating either as a direct measure of generated-code quality. This design makes it possible to examine whether relationships reported under a binary commit-rate definition remain monotonic when experience is represented as a multidimensional activity profile.
